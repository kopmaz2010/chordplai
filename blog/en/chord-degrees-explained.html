Chord degrees: learn the I–VII system once, play in every key

2026-07-29 · ChordPlai

If you memorize a song's chords as "Am, F, C, G", you only know that song in that one key. Think of it as "i–VI–III–VII" instead and you know it in every key. That's exactly what the degree system does: it names chords not by their letters, but by their role within the scale.

What is a degree?

A chord is built on each note of a scale, using only notes from that same scale. Whichever note of the scale it's built on gives the chord its degree. Uppercase (I, IV, V) means a major chord, lowercase (i, iv, v) means a minor chord, and the ° symbol marks a diminished chord.

The A minor example

DegreeChordQuality
iAmminor
ii°B°diminished
IIICmajor
ivDmminor
vEmminor
VIFmajor
VIIGmajor

These seven chords are the scale's diatonic chords: every one of them uses only notes from the A minor scale, which is why they sound good together in any order.

Why does it work so well?

  1. Transposing is free: To move the "i–VI–III–VII" pattern into E minor, you just change the scale: Em–C–G–D. The pattern itself is identical.
  2. Songs resemble each other: A huge share of popular music runs on a handful of degree patterns (I–V–vi–IV being the most famous). Once you recognize the pattern, you can figure out a new song on the first listen.
  3. Improvising gets easier: The degrees tell you which chord feels like "coming home" (i) and which ones create tension (V, VII).

Practice for training your ear on degrees

  • Pick a scale and loop the sequence i → iv → v → i; name the feeling each chord gives you.
  • Find the chords to a song you love, then write the degree next to each chord.
  • Play the same pattern in two different keys and confirm that you hear the same story.
